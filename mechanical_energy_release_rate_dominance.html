Why K controls the crack tip

Each zone represents a different physical regime around the crack tip. Understanding why the formula fails at the extremes reveals why K is so powerful in between.

02 — K as a funnel

All information passes through K before reaching the tip

The nonlinear zone has no direct access to the outside world. Everything — applied load, specimen shape, crack size — must pass through the K-dominated zone first. K is the only messenger.

Inputs (many)
Crack size, applied stress, specimen shape, boundary conditions — all different for every problem.
K condenses everything
Via K = ψ σA c½, all inputs are compressed into one number. The complexity disappears.
Nonlinear zone only sees K
The zone where fracture actually happens receives only K — never the raw inputs. So it responds identically whenever K is the same.

03 — Similitude principle

Same K = same crack tip conditions, always

This is the most powerful consequence. Two completely different specimens — different sizes, shapes, loading — present identical conditions to the nonlinear zone if their K values match.

Specimen A

Lab beam, c = 2 mm

Small four-point bend specimen. KI = 1.2 MPa√m. Compact geometry, controlled lab conditions.

Specimen B

Bridge girder, c = 40 mm

Massive structural element. Different crack size, different load, different geometry. But KI = 1.2 MPa√m.

Both present identical stress fields to the nonlinear zone KA = KB → same fracture behaviour
Key insight

This is why you can measure Kc in a small lab specimen and use it to predict failure in a massive real-world structure. The nonlinear zone never sees the difference — it only sees K.

04 — The stress field equation

What "K controls" means mathematically

At any point in the K-dominated zone, the stress is completely determined by K alone:

// Stress at distance r, angle θ from crack tip
σij(r, θ) = K / √(2πr) × fij(θ)

// K sets the intensity — same for all geometries
// f_ij(θ) is universal — same for all Mode I cracks

// At the nonlinear zone boundary r = r_min:
σboundary = K / √(2π·rmin) × fij(θ)
// → only K determines what the nonlinear zone experiences

06 — The critical requirement

Small-scale yielding condition

K-dominance only works if the nonlinear zone is much smaller than the K-dominated zone. This is the small-scale yielding condition you will meet in Chapter 3:

Condition met ✓
rnonlinear ≪ rK-zone
Nonlinear zone is tiny. K fully controls the boundary. Kc is a valid material property. Fracture mechanics works.
Condition violated ✗
rnonlinear ~ rK-zone
Nonlinear zone is large. K-dominated annulus is too thin. The framework breaks down — need elastic-plastic fracture mechanics (Chapter 3).
Chapter 2 assumption

Everything in Chapter 2 assumes small-scale yielding holds. For brittle solids (ceramics, glass), the nonlinear zone is sub-nanometre — the condition is satisfied extremely well. That is why the linear elastic framework works so beautifully for brittle fracture.
